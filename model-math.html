The Math

Pipeline order: ratings → serve-point probability → Markov match probability → calibration → EV. Calibration sits last because it decides whether the rest is worth anything.

01 Surface-weighted ELO

Expected result

Player A's expected score against B (1 = win, 0 = loss):

$$ E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}} $$

This is also a usable match win probability on its own.

Update after a match

$$ R_A' = R_A + K\,(S_A - E_A) $$

where \(S_A = 1\) if A won, \(0\) if A lost. B updates symmetrically (\(S_B = 1 - S_A\), \(E_B = 1 - E_A\)).

Dynamic K-factor

Don't use a fixed K. New players' ratings should move fast, veterans' slowly (FiveThirtyEight tennis form):

$$ K_i = \frac{250}{(m_i + 5)^{0.4}} $$

\(m_i\) = matches player \(i\) has played. Each player uses their own K.

Surface weighting

Keep an overall ELO and a surface-specific ELO (separate clay/grass/hard chains). The rating you feed the prediction is a blend:

$$ R_{\text{eff}} = w\, R_{\text{surface}} + (1 - w)\, R_{\text{overall}} $$

Tune \(w\) per surface — clay and grass are more specialized, so they take a higher \(w\), roughly \(0.5\text{–}0.6\). Tune on your own backtest; don't trust round numbers. Update both chains after every match.

02 Serve-point probability

The Markov model needs one number per player: the probability of winning a point on their own serve in this matchup. Decompose each player into serve skill and return skill (Barnett–Clarke).

  • \(f_t\) = tour-average fraction of points won on serve (≈ 0.62–0.65 on hard; lower on clay, higher on grass)
  • \(f_A\) = A's fraction of points won on serve
  • \(g_B\) = B's fraction of points won on return; tour-average return rate is \(1 - f_t\)

A's probability of winning a point while serving against B:

$$ p_A = f_t + (f_A - f_t) - \bigl(g_B - (1 - f_t)\bigr) $$

Start from tour average, add A's serving edge, subtract B's returning edge. Compute \(p_B\) with the roles flipped. Use surface-specific stats, or a surface multiplier if you're short on samples.

03 Markov match model

Turn \(p_A\) and \(p_B\) into a match probability. It nests: point → game → set → match.

Game — server holds

Let \(p\) be the server's point-win probability. Win 4 points, win by 2:

$$ P_{\text{game}}(p) = p^4 + 4p^4(1-p) + 10p^4(1-p)^2 + 20p^3(1-p)^3 \cdot \frac{p^2}{p^2 + (1-p)^2} $$

The terms are: win to love (4–0), to 15 (4–1), to 30 (4–2), and reach deuce (3–3) then win it. The deuce factor comes from \(P_{\text{deuce}} = p^2 + 2p(1-p)P_{\text{deuce}}\), which solves to \(\frac{p^2}{p^2 + (1-p)^2}\). So \(g_A = P_{\text{game}}(p_A)\) is A's hold probability and \(1 - P_{\text{game}}(p_B)\) is A's break probability.

Set — first to 6, win by 2, tiebreak at 6–6

No clean closed form, because serve alternates each game. Use a small dynamic program over (A games, B games, who serves):

$$ F(a, b, \text{server}) = \text{P(A wins the set from here)} $$

Transition on the current server's hold/break probability; the server flips each game. Terminals: A reaches 6 with a 2-game lead → 1; B does → 0; at (6,6) → the tiebreak value. Memoize — the state space is tiny.

Tiebreak — first to 7, win by 2

Same idea, separate DP over (A points, B points, server) with the 1–2–2 rotation. Once tied at 6–6 it becomes a deuce-style race whose value depends only on the two per-point win probabilities, which bounds the recursion cleanly. Call the resulting set probability \(s\).

Match

Treating sets as independent (a good approximation — carryover and fatigue are second order):

$$ \text{Best of 3:}\quad P_{\text{match}} = s^2(3 - 2s) $$ $$ \text{Best of 5:}\quad P_{\text{match}} = s^3\,(6s^2 - 15s + 10) $$

Scoreline probabilities fall out of the same expansion — the bonus of a mechanistic model: Bo3 gives \(P(2\text{–}0)=s^2\) and \(P(2\text{–}1)=2s^2(1-s)\); Bo5 gives \(P(3\text{–}0)=s^3\), \(P(3\text{–}1)=3s^3(1-s)\), \(P(3\text{–}2)=6s^3(1-s)^2\).

You now have two probabilities — ELO and Markov. They make different errors, so ensemble them. Calibrate the final output, not the pieces.

04 Turning probability into a bet

Strip the vig first

Decimal odds \(d_A, d_B\) give raw implied probabilities \(q_A = 1/d_A\), \(q_B = 1/d_B\) that sum to more than 1 — the excess is the bookmaker's margin. Remove it (proportional baseline):

$$ q_A^* = \frac{q_A}{q_A + q_B}, \qquad q_B^* = \frac{q_B}{q_A + q_B} $$

Shin and logarithmic de-vigging are better upgrades later.

Expected value

For a 1-unit bet on A at odds \(d_A\) given your calibrated probability \(p_A\):

$$ \text{EV} = p_A\, d_A - 1 $$

Bet only when \(\text{EV} > 0\), i.e. \(p_A > 1/d_A\). Your edge is that value.

Stake sizing — fractional Kelly

$$ f^* = \frac{p\,d - 1}{d - 1} = \frac{bp - q}{b}, \quad b = d - 1,\; q = 1 - p $$

Full Kelly assumes your probabilities are exactly right — they aren't, so scale down by \(\lambda\):

$$ \text{stake} = \lambda \, f^* \cdot (\text{bankroll}), \qquad \lambda \in [0.25,\, 0.5] $$

05 Calibration — the part that has to be right

You cannot make calibration permanently right and forget it. A model is calibrated on a dataset, at a moment, for a segment. It drifts as the tour changes, players age, and surfaces play differently year to year. "Always right" means continuously measured and re-fit.

For betting this matters more than accuracy. A model that's 70% accurate but says "90%" when it means "70%" hands you positive-EV signals that are actually negative-EV, and Kelly sizes into them hard. Calibration is what makes the EV math trustworthy.

Step 1 — measure it

Reliability diagram: bin predictions (10 bins of 0.1), plot mean predicted probability against observed win frequency. Perfect calibration sits on the diagonal; points below it mean overconfident. Expected Calibration Error summarizes the diagram:

$$ \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{N}\,\bigl|\,\text{acc}(b) - \text{conf}(b)\,\bigr| $$

Brier score (overall probabilistic accuracy, lower is better):

$$ \text{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i - o_i)^2, \qquad o_i \in \{0,1\} $$

Log loss punishes confident-and-wrong hardest — exactly the betting failure mode:

$$ \text{LogLoss} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl[\,o_i \log p_i + (1 - o_i)\log(1 - p_i)\,\bigr] $$

Step 2 — fix it

Fit the calibration map on a held-out set — not training, not test. Platt scaling (the robust default) fits two scalars on the model's log-odds \(z = \log\frac{p}{1-p}\):

$$ p_{\text{cal}} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(A z + B))} $$

Isotonic regression fits any non-decreasing map — more flexible, needs more data, overfits small sets. Beta calibration works on \([0,1]\) directly and handles the typical S-shaped miscalibration better; a good later upgrade.

Step 3 — keep it right

  1. Three-way split. Train, calibrate, test stay separate. Calibrating on training data gives a fake-perfect diagram.
  2. Calibrate per segment. A model can be calibrated overall and badly off on clay, on Bo5, or on big underdogs. Slice by surface, tour, tier, and favorite/underdog.
  3. Watch the tails. Heavy favorites (\(p > 0.9\)) and big dogs break first and hide longshot value. Bin them finely.
  4. Re-fit on a rolling window. Recompute periodically; a rising ECE is your signal to re-fit.
  5. Calibrate the final ensemble, after blending — not the components.

Backtesting guards

  • No look-ahead. All ratings/stats strictly as-of pre-match. The most common way to fool yourself.
  • Test against the closing line, not opening odds — it's the sharpest market estimate, and beating it is the real proof of edge.
  • Apply vig and realistic fills, not theoretical fair odds.
  • Check ROI stability, not just total profit — a backtest carried by three longshots isn't an edge.

→ Build order

  1. Surface-weighted ELO + dynamic K
  2. Logistic regression on features (ELO diff, serve/return, form, fatigue) as a benchmark
  3. Markov serve model for mechanistic probabilities + scorelines
  4. Ensemble the two
  5. Calibrate the output and verify per-segment before risking a cent
  6. Backtest against closing lines with vig applied
  7. De-vig live odds → EV → fractional Kelly

Get steps 1, 2, and 5 working end to end before anything fancy. A calibrated simple model beats an uncalibrated sophisticated one every time.

For real-money use: a real market is sharper than a home model, edges are thin and decay, and no calibration holds forever. Treat responsible-gambling tooling — limits, loss alerts, self-exclusion — as core features, not afterthoughts.
